### Free Hard Water Test

A hard water test measures calcium and magnesium in your water, reported in milligrams per liter or grains per gallon. One grain per gallon equals about 17.1 mg/L. The U.S. Geological Survey rates water under 60 mg/L as soft, 61 to 120 as moderately hard, 121 to 180 as hard and over 180 as very hard. <mark class="data-gap">[NEEDS DATA: which tests are free and what the free test includes]</mark> The result decides what size of softener fits your home.

### TDS Testing

Total dissolved solids (TDS) is the combined amount of minerals, salts and metals dissolved in water, in milligrams per liter. The EPA's secondary guideline for TDS is 500 mg/L, based on taste and appearance rather than health. A high reading points to reverse osmosis or a mineral-focused treatment.

### Iron Content Testing

An iron test checks for dissolved and particulate iron. The EPA's secondary level is 0.3 mg/L, above which water may stain fixtures and taste metallic. Iron affects softener choice, because high iron fouls resin without a filter ahead of it.

### pH Level Testing

pH measures how acidic or basic water is. The EPA's secondary range is 6.5 to 8.5. Low pH can corrode pipes and fixtures, and high pH can cause scale and bitter taste. pH also guides the choice of media in some filters.

### Chlorine Testing

A chlorine test measures the disinfectant left in your tap water. It helps explain taste and odor and guides carbon filter choices. Lubbock's water is disinfected with chlorine or chloramine at roughly 1.0 to 2.0 mg/L, so a chlorine test usually reads in that range.

### Sulfur Testing

A sulfur test confirms hydrogen sulfide, the cause of a rotten-egg smell, most common in private wells. The result decides whether you need an oxidizing filter.

### Heavy Metals Testing

Heavy metal tests screen for lead, arsenic and similar metals. These need a certified lab and are most useful for older plumbing, private wells and families with infants. Results guide whether a contaminant filter or reverse osmosis is justified.

### City vs. Well Water Testing in Lubbock

City water and well water need different testing. City of Lubbock water quality reports show that roughly 60 to 70 percent of the supply comes from the Ogallala Aquifer, and the city treats and tests that water before it reaches homes. Private wells are not tested for you, and well-water guidance recommends testing for bacteria every year, with nitrate and other substances on a schedule that depends on local conditions. A tap test shows what reaches your home, whichever source it comes from. City of Lubbock Water Utilities reports average hardness at approximately 205 mg/L, or 12 grains per gallon, which the U.S. Geological Survey rates as very hard. <mark class="data-gap">[NEEDS DATA: typical well results]</mark>

## What should you test for first?

Start with hardness, TDS and pH, which cover the common local issues. Add iron and sulfur for wells or staining, and add heavy metals when plumbing is old or there is an infant in the home.

**How often should I test my water?**
Test city water when you notice a change or move in. Test a private well for bacteria at least once a year, and for other substances as local conditions suggest.

**Do I need a test before buying a softener?**
Yes. The result sizes the softener and shows whether you also need a filter.

**What test shows if my water is safe to drink?**
A certified lab panel for bacteria, nitrate, metals and other regulated substances. City of Lubbock reports cover the public supply.
